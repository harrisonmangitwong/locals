## Design Context

### Users
- **Primary**: NYC residents looking for authentic neighborhood restaurants, not tourist traps. They know their city, they want data-backed picks that match their taste, and they want to discover spots in neighborhoods they haven't explored yet.
- **Secondary**: Visitors to NYC who want to eat where locals eat, not where TripAdvisor sends them.
- **Tertiary**: Recruiters and hiring managers viewing this as a portfolio project. The design needs to signal "real product" not "weekend hackathon."
- **Context**: Mobile-first browsing (texting a friend "where should we eat?"), desktop for deeper exploration. Decision-making moment is urgent — they're hungry now or planning tonight.

### Brand Personality
- **Voice**: Warm, trustworthy, local
- **Tone**: Like a knowledgeable friend who lives in the neighborhood. Not corporate, not pretentious, not try-hard. Confident but approachable. Says "this place is legit" not "our algorithm has determined optimal dining establishments."
- **Emotional goals**: Trust (these recommendations are real), discovery (I found something I wouldn't have found otherwise), belonging (I'm eating where real New Yorkers eat)

### Aesthetic Direction
- **Visual tone**: Editorial and photography-forward, like The Infatuation or Eater. Strong typography that carries personality. Food photos are the hero, not UI chrome.
- **References**: The Infatuation (editorial authority, strong type, photography-first), Eater (opinionated curation, clean layouts, distinctive voice in design)
- **Anti-references**: Generic AI templates (dark mode + gradient text + glassmorphism + emoji icons), Yelp (cluttered, ad-heavy), TripAdvisor (tourist-oriented, dated)
- **Theme**: System-aware (light and dark mode). Light mode primary — food photos pop better on light backgrounds, and it breaks from the AI-dark-mode default. Dark mode as a polished alternative.
- **Color direction**: Move away from the Airbnb-coral #ff385c. Find a palette that feels warm and neighborhood-y, not startup-generic. Consider earthy tones, warm neutrals, or a distinctive accent that isn't coral/pink/purple.
- **Typography**: Inter is fine as a workhorse, but consider a display face with more personality for headings — something that signals editorial authority without being decorative.
- **Icons**: No emoji as icons. Use a clean icon set (Lucide, Heroicons) or no icons at all — let typography and photography carry the design.

### Design Principles

1. **Photography first** — The food photo is the most important element on every card and page. UI should frame and support the photo, never compete with it. If a design choice makes the photo less prominent, it's wrong.

2. **Earned authority, not decoration** — Every visual element should communicate something useful. No gradient text, no glassmorphism, no decorative badges. The product earns trust through clear data presentation and honest recommendations, not visual flair.

3. **Neighborhood feel** — The design should feel like it belongs to NYC, not to Silicon Valley. Warm, lived-in, specific. Not sleek-corporate, not startup-playful. Think bodega receipt, not pitch deck.

4. **Show, don't chrome** — Minimize UI chrome (borders, backgrounds, containers). Let content breathe. Use whitespace and typography hierarchy instead of boxes and dividers to create structure.

5. **Respect the decision** — Users are making a real choice (where to eat). Surface the information that matters (location, cuisine, why it's good, price) clearly and quickly. Don't make them work for it.
